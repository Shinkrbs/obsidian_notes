**What is Research?**
- A systematic, disciplined process of inquiry aimed at discovering, interpreting, and revising knowledge.
- Produces verifiable, replicable findings rather than opinions or assumptions
- Driven by a question that existing knowledge cannot fully answer
- Follow a structured method, not improvised or arbitrary
- Produces an evidence-based conclusions that others can examine and challenge
- Documented-findings are communicated through reports, papers, or presentations
- Aims for generalizability, results should extend beyond the specific study sample

**Why Research matters in IT/CS?**
- Advances algorithms, systems and computing theory
- Validates whether a new technology actually works and how it went well
- Guides industry standards and best practices
- Justifies technology choices with data, not preference
- Enables informed problem-solving under uncertainty
- Build credibility in professional and academic contexts
- Prepares you to evaluate other's claims critically

**Types of Research in IT/CS**
- Basic Research
- Applied Research
- Development Research

***Basic Research***
- Expands fundamental knowledge without immediate application in mind
- Driven by curiosity and theoretical interest
- Example: Proving the computation limits of a new complexity class; developing a new cryptographic primitive

***Applied Research***
- Uses existing theory to solve a specific, real-world problem
- Produces actionable outputs
- Example: Measuring the accuracy of a ML model on local healthcare data; optimizing a network topology

***Development Research***
- Design, builds, and evaluates a system, tool, or process
- Example: Building and evaluating a mobile attendance monitoring system

***Quantitative Research***
- Data Form: Numbers, Measurements, Metrics
- Goal: Measure, Compare, Predict
- Tools: Surveys (Likert), Sensors, Logs
- Analysis: Statistical Methods
- Example: Measuring the response time of two APIs

***Qualitative Research***
- Data Form: Text, Narratives, Observations
- Goal: Understand, Interpret, Describe
- Tools: Interviews, Focus Group Discussions, Case Studies
- Analysis: Thematic, content, discourse analysis
- Example: Studying how developers describe debugging frustration

***Scientific Thinking***
- **Empericism**: Knowledge must be grounded in observable, measurable evidence, not authority or intution 
- **Skepticism**: All claims are provisional; they must survive scrutiny and replication before acceptance
- **Objectivity**: Findings are shaped by evidence, not by the researcher's preferences or desired outcomes.
- **Systematic Inquiry**: Questions are investigated through defined, repeatable procedure
- **Falsifiability**: A scientific claim must be stated in a way that allows it to be proven wrong

***Scientific Method***
- **Observe**: Notice a phenomenon, pattern, or gap in practice or knowledge
- **Ask**: Formulate a focused, answerable research question
- **Hypothesize**: Propose a testable explanation or prediction
- **Experiment/Collect**: Gather data using a defined method
- **Analyze**: Apply statistical or logical methods to the data
- **Conclude**: Accept, or reject the hypothesis based on the findings
- **Communicate**: Report findings so others can evaluate and replicate them

***Research Problem***
- A clearly stated gap between what is currently known (how things currently work) and what need to be known (or improved)
- It justifies the existence of the study

***Sources of Research Problem***
- An observed performance gap; a system is slower, less accurate or more prone to errors than expected
- A literature gap - existing studies did not test a particular variable, context or population
- A practical need - an organization lacks a tool that could solve a real operational problem
- A contradictory finding - two existing studies reach opposing conclusions
- A new technology - an emerging framework, algorithm, or device has not yet been evaluated in your context
*Example*
- Observation: Students frequently reported that the Wi-Fi frequently drops during peak hours
- Problem Statement: Network reliability during peak load is insufficient; root causes and user impact are unquantified
- Research question: What factors significantly predict Wi-Fi disconnection frequency in the university on the library setting?
- Hypothesis: Concurrent user count and time of day are significant predictors of the disconnection frequency
- Method: Collect a 30-day network data logs; appl regression analysis to identify predictors

***Characteristics of a Good Research Problem***
- **Feasible**: can be investigated within available time, budget, and data access
- **Specific**: Narrow enough to be answerable; not a broad topic area
- **Significant**: Findings will contribute to knowledge or practice
- **Ethical**: Does not harm participants or violate data privacy
- **Novel**: Adds something not already conclusively answered in existing literature
- **Measurable**: Key variables can be observed and quantified
- **Researchable**: Can be investigated through empirical means, not opinion or belief
