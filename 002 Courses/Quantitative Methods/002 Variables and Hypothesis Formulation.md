**What is a variable?**
- Any characteristic, attribute, or quantity that can take on different values across observations in a study.
- If something cannot vary, its a constant not a variable

**Variables in Quantitative Research**
- Must be operationally defined: specified in measurable and observable terms
- Must be measurable using a defined scale (**nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio***)
- Must be clearly labeled so others can replicate the measurement procedure
- Example: User satisfaction: the mean score on a 5-point Likert Scale

**Three types of Variables**
- Independent Variable
- Dependent Variable
- Control Variable

**Independent Variable**
- The variable that the researcher manipulates or selects to observe its effect
- It is the presumed cause in the cause-effect relationship
- It is called as the predictor variable, treatment variable or explanatory variable

**Dependent Variable**
- The variable that is observed and measured as the outcome
- It is the presumed effect that may change in response to the independent variable
- Also called as the outcome variable, response variable, criterion variable
- Example: Test score, classification accuracy, execution time, user error rate

**Control Variable**
- Variables that are held constant or accounted for so they do not confound the relationship between independent and dependent variable
- Also called the confounding variable, covariate
- Example: device type, internet speed

**Tips in Identifying Variables**
- Independent Variable: What am I changing?
- Dependent Variable: What am I measuring as a result?
- Control variable: What else could affect the dependent variable that I am not studying?

**What is a hypothesis?
- A precise, testable statement that predicts the relationship between two or more variables
- It must be falsifiable - capable of being shown to be wrong by empirical data

**Properties of a good hypothesis**
- State the expected relationship between the independent and dependent variable
- It is written in a declarative sentence, not a question
- It is testable: the relationship can be confirmed or refuted with data
- It is specific: the names of population and variables are precisely defined
- It is grounded by a literature review: not guessed randomly

**Hypothesis Pair**
- In quantitative research: hypothesis always come in pairs: null (H0/Ho) and alternative (H1/Ha)

**Null Hypothesis**
- The default claim that there is no significant relationship, difference or effect between the variables being studied

**Key Characteristics of Null Hypothesis**
- Always state no effect, no difference, or no relationship
- It i the statement that statistical tests are designed to evaluate directly
- When rejecting the Ho, "Reject the null hypothesis"
- When accepting the Ho, "Failed to reject the null hypothesis" not ''accepted'' or "proven"
- Represents what we would conclude by default if the data are inconclusive

**The Alternative Hypothesis**
- It claims that there is significant relationship difference, or effect. 
- It is what the researcher typically hopes the data will support

**Key Characteristics of Alternative Hypothesis**
- Directly  contradicts the null hypothesis
- Can be directional (predicts which group scores is higher) or non-directional (predicts a difference, but now which direction)
- It is supported when the null hypothesis is rejected
- Must be grounded in prior literature or theory, not wishful thinking

**Directional Hypothesis**
- Predicts the direction of the effect: specifies which group will the score be higher or which variable will increase/decrease
- Use when prior literature strongly supports a specific direction

**Non-directional Hypothesis**
- Predicts that a difference or relationship exists but does not specify which direction
- More conservative and common in exploratory work

**Steps in Conducting a Research**
- Step 1: Identify the research problem. What gap or question is being investigated?
- Step 2: Name the variables, Identify the IV, DV, and CV; operationally defined each variable
- Step 3: Review the literature. What direction does prior evidence suggest?
- Step 4: Write the H0. State the no effect claim.
- Step 5: Write the H1. State the effect claim; choose directional or non-directional
